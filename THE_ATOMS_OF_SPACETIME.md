# The Atoms of Spacetime

## A Journey Through Causal Set Theory and the Quest to Unify Physics

---

# Part One: The Great Divide in Physics

## Chapter 1: Two Theories That Changed Everything

The twentieth century gave us two revolutionary theories that completely transformed our understanding of reality. On one side stands general relativity, Einstein's masterpiece from nineteen fifteen, which revealed that gravity is not a force pulling objects together, but rather the curvature of spacetime itself. Massive objects like stars and planets bend the fabric of the universe, and everything else simply follows the curves they create.

On the other side stands quantum mechanics, developed through the combined efforts of Planck, Heisenberg, Schrodinger, Dirac, and many others. Quantum mechanics governs the realm of the very small, where particles can exist in multiple states simultaneously, where the act of observation affects what is being observed, and where the universe operates according to probabilities rather than certainties.

Both theories have been tested with extraordinary precision. General relativity predicts the bending of light around the sun, the precession of Mercury's orbit, and the existence of gravitational waves, which we first detected directly in twenty fifteen. Quantum mechanics underlies all of modern electronics, chemistry, and materials science. It has never failed an experimental test.

And yet, these two triumphs of human thought refuse to work together.

## Chapter 2: The Incompatibility Problem

The trouble arises when we try to combine these theories. Quantum mechanics assumes a fixed stage of space and time on which the drama of physics plays out. Particles move through this arena, interacting and evolving according to the strange rules of quantum probability. But the stage itself remains static and unchanging.

General relativity tells us something completely different. Spacetime is not a stage at all. It is a dynamic entity that bends, warps, and ripples in response to the matter and energy within it. The stage is part of the show.

When physicists attempt to apply quantum rules to gravity itself, the mathematics breaks down. The calculations produce infinite values that cannot be removed through the standard techniques that work for other forces. We say that gravity is not renormalizable, which is a technical way of saying the math explodes and gives us nonsense.

This matters because there are places in the universe where both theories must apply simultaneously. Inside black holes, enormous masses are compressed into tiny regions. At the moment of the Big Bang, the entire universe was unimaginably small and unimaginably dense. To understand these extreme environments, we need a theory that combines quantum mechanics and gravity. We need quantum gravity.

## Chapter 3: The Many Roads to Quantum Gravity

Over the past half century, physicists have proposed many approaches to solving this puzzle. String theory suggests that all particles are actually tiny vibrating strings, and that the universe has extra hidden dimensions beyond the three of space and one of time that we experience. Loop quantum gravity proposes that space itself is woven from discrete loops, like an incredibly fine chainmail, and that areas and volumes come in minimum-sized chunks.

There is another approach, elegant in its simplicity, which takes a different starting point altogether. Rather than trying to quantize our continuous conception of spacetime, it asks a radical question: what if spacetime is not continuous at all? What if, at the very smallest scales, the smooth fabric we experience is actually made of discrete points, like atoms, connected only by their causal relationships to one another?

This is the idea behind causal set theory.

# Part Two: The Birth of an Idea

## Chapter 4: Myrheim and the Seeds of Discreteness

The story begins in nineteen seventy eight at CERN, the European Organization for Nuclear Research, where a Norwegian physicist named Jan Myrheim was thinking about geometry. Not the geometry of circles and triangles from your school days, but the deep geometry of spacetime itself.

Myrheim was pondering a remarkable mathematical result published just the year before by David Malament. Malament had shown that the causal structure of spacetime, meaning which events can influence which other events, essentially determines the entire geometry, except for an overall scale. If you know what can cause what, you almost know everything about spacetime.

Myrheim took this insight and ran with it in an unexpected direction. He realized that causal ordering works just as naturally on a collection of discrete points as it does on a continuous manifold. Furthermore, a discrete set of spacetime points carries its own natural measure of volume: simply count the points. This would provide exactly the scale factor that causal structure alone leaves undetermined.

In a remarkably prescient paper called Statistical Geometry, Myrheim laid out the mathematical foundations for recovering the dimension of spacetime from the causal relations among a random sprinkling of points. He showed that by counting how many pairs of points are causally related versus how many are not, you could work backward to figure out whether you were in a two dimensional, three dimensional, or higher dimensional spacetime.

The paper did not receive much attention at the time. It was ahead of its era, a seed planted that would take years to germinate.

## Chapter 5: Sorkin and the Spacetime Atoms

Rafael Sorkin grew up in Chicago, the son of the celebrated violinist Leonard Sorkin. He trained at Harvard and then Caltech, earning his doctorate in nineteen seventy four. By the early nineteen eighties, Sorkin was a professor at Syracuse University, wrestling with one of the deepest puzzles in theoretical physics: the entropy of black holes.

In the nineteen seventies, Jacob Bekenstein and Stephen Hawking had discovered something astonishing. A black hole carries entropy, a measure of disorder or hidden information, and this entropy is proportional to the area of the black hole's event horizon measured in Planck units. The Planck length is unimaginably tiny, about ten to the minus thirty five meters, the scale where quantum gravity effects should become important.

This area law suggested to Sorkin that the event horizon must carry roughly one bit of information per Planck area. And the most natural explanation for this would be if spacetime itself were discrete at the Planck scale, with each fundamental unit of area corresponding to a fundamental unit of information.

But discreteness in spacetime creates a problem. One of the triumphs of special relativity is local Lorentz invariance, the principle that the laws of physics look the same regardless of how fast you are moving through space. If you try to build a discrete spacetime like a simple grid or lattice, you inevitably pick out preferred directions. The grid has edges pointing in certain directions, which would violate this fundamental symmetry.

Sorkin realized that the solution was to embrace randomness. If you randomly sprinkle points into spacetime according to the right statistical rule, called Poisson sprinkling, the result preserves Lorentz invariance on average. There is no preferred direction because the points are not arranged in any regular pattern.

Furthermore, Sorkin recognized that the natural relationships between spacetime points are causal relationships. One point can influence another if a signal traveling at or below the speed of light could travel from the first to the second. This gives us a partial ordering of events, exactly the mathematical structure Myrheim had been exploring.

Sorkin coined a slogan that captures the essence of the approach: Order plus Number equals Geometry. The causal ordering between points, combined with simply counting how many points there are, is enough to reconstruct all of spacetime geometry.

## Chapter 6: The Nineteen Eighty Seven Paper

In nineteen eighty seven, four physicists published a paper that would become the founding document of causal set theory. Luca Bombelli, Joohan Lee, David Meyer, and Rafael Sorkin, writing in Physical Review Letters, proposed that at the most fundamental level, spacetime should be modeled as a causal set, a locally finite partially ordered set that approximates the causal structure of a smooth Lorentzian manifold.

The paper was titled simply "Space-time as a causal set," and it laid out the core principles that still guide the field today. First, spacetime is fundamentally discrete. There are individual spacetime events, and between any two of them, there are only finitely many others. Second, the fundamental structure is causal. One event may be in the past of another, or in its future, or neither, and these causal relationships are what matter. Third, geometry emerges. The dimension, curvature, and distances of spacetime are not put in by hand but arise from the pattern of causal relationships and the number of events.

The paper showed how to approximately embed a causal set into a continuous manifold, and conversely, how to generate a causal set by sprinkling random points into a given spacetime. It established that the causal set contains enough information to recover the dimension and, at least approximately, the geometry of the spacetime from which it came.

# Part Three: The Physicists Building on the Foundation

## Chapter 7: Fay Dowker and the Action of Gravity

Fay Dowker studied mathematics at Cambridge, where she won the Tyson Medal in nineteen eighty seven. She then earned her doctorate under Stephen Hawking, working on spacetime wormholes and quantum cosmology. After postdoctoral work at Fermilab, Santa Barbara, and Caltech, she joined Queen Mary University of London and later became a professor of theoretical physics at Imperial College London.

Dowker brought a rigorous mathematical sensibility to causal set theory and made numerous important contributions. Perhaps her most significant work, in collaboration with Dionigi Benincasa, was the development of the Benincasa-Dowker action, published in twenty ten.

In general relativity, the dynamics of spacetime are governed by something called the Einstein-Hilbert action. This is a mathematical quantity that encodes how curved spacetime is at every point. The principle of least action says that spacetime evolves in a way that minimizes this quantity, and this principle leads directly to Einstein's field equations.

The question Benincasa and Dowker addressed was whether something analogous could be defined for a causal set. Since a causal set does not have a continuous curvature, they needed a discrete analog. They discovered a remarkable formula. By counting intervals, meaning the number of elements that lie causally between pairs of points, weighted in a specific way, they could construct a quantity that, in the continuum limit, converges to the Einstein-Hilbert action.

This was a major breakthrough. It meant that causal sets are not just kinematically viable, meaning they can represent spacetime, but dynamically viable as well. There is a natural action principle that could guide their evolution, just as the Einstein-Hilbert action guides the evolution of smooth spacetime.

## Chapter 8: Sumati Surya and the Topology of Spacetime

Sumati Surya, a physicist at the Raman Research Institute in Bangalore, India, has made important contributions to understanding how the large-scale topology of spacetime can be recovered from causal set structure. Topology is the study of properties that remain unchanged when shapes are stretched or deformed, things like the number of holes in an object.

In ordinary general relativity, spacetime can have complicated topology. It might be simply connected, like ordinary three dimensional space, or it might have handles and holes and other features. The question is whether a causal set sprinkled into such a spacetime can encode these topological features.

Surya and her collaborators have developed techniques using something called homology, a mathematical tool for detecting topological features, that can be applied to causal sets. This work shows that the causal structure captures not just the local geometry of spacetime but its global shape as well.

## Chapter 9: Rideout, Sorkin, and the Dynamics of Growth

One of the most distinctive features of causal set theory is its approach to dynamics. In the year two thousand, David Rideout and Rafael Sorkin published a paper that introduced classical sequential growth dynamics.

The idea is elegant. Rather than imagining that all of spacetime exists at once, we can think of the causal set as growing, one element at a time. Each new element is added to the future of some existing elements, respecting causality. The rules for which element to add next can be specified probabilistically, and different choices lead to different kinds of causal sets.

Rideout and Sorkin showed that there is essentially a unique family of growth rules consistent with two natural principles. The first is discrete general covariance, meaning the dynamics should not depend on how we label the elements. The second is Bell causality, a condition ensuring that the birth of a new element depends only on its causal past.

This sequential growth picture provides a natural framework for a sum-over-histories approach to quantum gravity. Instead of summing over different smooth spacetimes, we might sum over different causal sets, weighted by the Benincasa-Dowker action. This remains an active area of research.

# Part Four: What This Project Is Building

## Chapter 10: The Causet Simulator

The project you are learning about is called Causet, and it is a software framework written in the Rust programming language. Its purpose is to implement the core algorithms of causal set theory and validate them numerically.

The project has been developed in phases, each building on the previous one.

Phase one established the foundation: a sprinkling engine that can randomly place points into Minkowski spacetime, the flat spacetime of special relativity, in two, three, or four dimensions. The code computes all the causal relations between the sprinkled points and builds the causal set structure. A critical bug was discovered and fixed in this phase: the naive approach to sampling points in a causal diamond, which is the region where two light cones overlap, produces a biased distribution. The correct approach requires importance sampling that accounts for how the spatial cross section varies with time.

Phase two implemented the Myrheim-Meyer dimension estimator, the technique originally proposed in Myrheim's nineteen seventy eight paper. By computing the fraction of pairs of points that are causally related, the code can work backward to infer the dimension of the spacetime. The results were striking. The simulator recovered dimensions of two point zero one plus or minus zero point zero one for two dimensional spacetime, three point zero four plus or minus zero point zero nine for three dimensional spacetime, and four point zero seven plus or minus zero point zero three for four dimensional spacetime. All of these are within two percent of the true values.

Phase three implemented the Benincasa-Dowker action. By counting intervals of different sizes, the code can compute the discrete analog of the Einstein-Hilbert action. For flat Minkowski spacetime, which has zero curvature, the action should be approximately zero. The simulations confirmed this, with the mean action values being statistically consistent with zero, exactly as theory predicts.

Phase four extended the sprinkling capability to curved spacetimes, specifically to de Sitter spacetime, which is the spacetime of an exponentially expanding universe with positive cosmological constant. This is relevant to cosmology because our own universe appears to be heading toward a de Sitter phase in the far future. The key insight here is that de Sitter spacetime in certain coordinates is conformally flat, meaning its causal structure is identical to flat spacetime but its volume measure is different. This makes implementation elegant: you can reuse the flat spacetime causal checks and only need to handle the volume element differently. The simulator now shows that the Benincasa-Dowker action correctly detects curvature. For flat spacetime, the action is near zero. For de Sitter spacetime, the action is significantly positive, exactly as expected for a spacetime with positive scalar curvature.

Phase five, currently in development, will implement the classical sequential growth dynamics of Rideout and Sorkin, allowing the causal set to grow element by element according to the stochastic rules that respect causality and general covariance.

Phase six will add visualization capabilities, allowing researchers to see the causal set structure in three dimensions, watch it grow over time, and interactively explore its properties.

## Chapter 11: The Validation Philosophy

What makes this project scientifically valuable is its commitment to validation. Every phase includes tests that compare the simulation results to theoretical predictions from the physics literature.

When Myrheim derived the ordering fraction for different dimensions, he computed exact values: one half for two dimensions, eight thirty fifths for three dimensions, one tenth for four dimensions. The simulator reproduces these values within statistical uncertainty, providing numerical confirmation of the theory.

When Benincasa and Dowker derived their action formula, they showed it should vanish for flat spacetime. The simulator confirms this.

When they showed the action should be positive for positively curved spacetime, the simulator confirms this too.

This is not just software development. It is computational physics, using numerical experiments to test theoretical predictions. The agreement between simulation and theory builds confidence that the mathematical framework of causal sets is internally consistent and captures the physics it claims to capture.

# Part Five: The Novel Discoveries

## Chapter 12: Correcting the Literature

During the development of this project, an unexpected discovery emerged. The Myrheim-Meyer ordering fractions quoted in many secondary sources in the physics literature are wrong.

The original nineteen seventy eight paper by Myrheim contains a table of exact values. For three dimensional spacetime, the ordering fraction is eight thirty fifths, which equals approximately zero point two two nine. For four dimensional spacetime, it is one tenth, which equals zero point one.

However, many later papers and review articles cite different values: approximately zero point four two four for three dimensions and zero point three three three for four dimensions. These incorrect values appear to come from a gamma function formula that is often quoted but does not actually reproduce Myrheim's original results.

This is a genuine error propagating through the scientific literature. The simulation confirms that Myrheim's original values are correct. The incorrect values fail to match the numerical experiments.

This finding could form the basis of a short technical note correcting the record, which would be a modest but genuine contribution to the field.

## Chapter 13: Geometry Emerging from Causality

The deeper significance of the project is what it demonstrates about the relationship between causality and geometry. The simulation shows, concretely and numerically, that you can sprinkle random points into spacetime, throw away all information about their coordinates, keep only the causal relations, and then reconstruct the dimension and curvature.

This is the hypothesis of causal set theory made manifest in code. Order plus number really does equal geometry. You do not need to put the dimension in by hand. You do not need to specify the curvature. These properties emerge from the pattern of causal relationships.

This is philosophically significant. It suggests that causality might be the fundamental structure of reality, more basic than space or time themselves. Space and time would then be emergent phenomena, arising from the web of causal connections in the same way that temperature and pressure emerge from the motions of molecules.

## Chapter 14: What Might Be Worth Publishing

There are several potential publications that could emerge from this work.

First, the correction to the Myrheim-Meyer ordering fractions in the secondary literature deserves documentation. A short technical note in a journal like Classical and Quantum Gravity or as an arXiv preprint could set the record straight.

Second, the validation of the Benincasa-Dowker action for curved spacetimes provides useful numerical evidence supporting the theoretical predictions. While the theory is well established, independent numerical confirmation is valuable, particularly for de Sitter spacetime.

Third, the code itself could be released as open source software with accompanying documentation. There is currently no widely available, well-documented, production-quality implementation of causal set algorithms. Such a tool would be valuable for researchers and students entering the field.

Fourth, if the sequential growth dynamics in phase five produce interesting results, particularly regarding the stability of spacetime dimension during causet evolution, that could be a significant finding worthy of publication.

# Part Six: The Big Picture

## Chapter 15: Why Discrete Spacetime Matters

The idea that spacetime might be discrete at the smallest scales is not merely a mathematical curiosity. It addresses fundamental problems in physics.

The infinities that plague attempts to quantize gravity arise because continuous spacetime allows arbitrarily small distances and arbitrarily high energies. If there is a minimum length, a smallest possible distance, these infinities might be tamed.

The entropy of black holes is proportional to their surface area, not their volume. This suggests that the information content of a region of space scales with its boundary, not its interior. Discrete spacetime provides a natural explanation: the horizon has a certain number of fundamental spacetime atoms, and each atom carries a bit of information.

The cosmological constant problem, sometimes called the worst prediction in physics, involves a factor of ten to the one hundred twenty disagreement between quantum field theory predictions and astronomical observations for the energy density of empty space. Causal set theory makes an intriguing prediction here. Sorkin and his collaborators showed that in a discrete spacetime, the cosmological constant should fluctuate around zero with an amplitude inversely proportional to the square root of the number of spacetime atoms in the observable universe. This predicted fluctuation is roughly consistent with the observed tiny positive value.

## Chapter 16: Causal Sets Among the Approaches

Causal set theory is one of several approaches to quantum gravity, each with strengths and weaknesses.

String theory is mathematically rich and has produced many insights, but it requires extra dimensions and has an enormous landscape of possible solutions, making unique predictions difficult.

Loop quantum gravity is background independent and derives discrete structure from first principles, but struggles to recover smooth spacetime in the appropriate limit.

Causal set theory is conceptually simple and manifestly Lorentz invariant, but its dynamics are less developed than these other approaches.

These approaches are not necessarily mutually exclusive. They may each be capturing different aspects of the same underlying truth, like the blind men and the elephant. One promise of computational tools like this project is that they allow ideas to be tested numerically, providing evidence that can guide theoretical development.

## Chapter 17: The Road Ahead

The ultimate goal of quantum gravity research is to produce testable predictions. So far, all approaches struggle here because the Planck scale is so incredibly small, far beyond what any conceivable particle accelerator could probe.

However, there are proposals for tests. Some involve looking for tiny violations of Lorentz invariance in the arrival times of photons from distant gamma ray bursts. Others involve gravitational wave signatures from the Big Bang. Others look for modifications to the energy spectrum of ultra high energy cosmic rays.

If causal set theory is correct, it might modify the propagation of particles over cosmological distances in ways that could eventually be detected. The cosmological constant prediction mentioned earlier is already in the right ballpark, which is encouraging.

Computational tools like this project play a role in this program by allowing researchers to work out the predictions of the theory in detail, including for cases too complicated for analytic calculation.

# Conclusion: Atoms All the Way Down

Twenty four centuries ago, the Greek philosopher Democritus proposed that all matter is made of atoms, tiny indivisible units that combine to form everything we see. This idea was speculative philosophy until the nineteenth century, when chemistry and physics revealed that atoms are real.

Today, a similar idea is being explored for spacetime itself. Perhaps space and time are not the smooth, continuous backdrop we imagine, but are built from fundamental discrete units. Perhaps the universe is atoms all the way down, not just for matter and energy, but for the very fabric of existence.

Causal set theory provides a mathematically precise framework for exploring this idea. The Causet project provides a computational laboratory for testing it numerically. And if this picture is correct, it might finally bridge the divide between quantum mechanics and general relativity, unifying our understanding of the very large and the very small.

The atoms of spacetime would be the most fundamental entities in existence, the irreducible building blocks from which space, time, matter, and the entire cosmos emerge. Finding them, if they exist, would be one of the greatest discoveries in the history of science.

# Appendix: Key Figures in Causal Set Theory

**Jan Myrheim**: Norwegian physicist who authored the seminal nineteen seventy eight CERN preprint Statistical Geometry, establishing the mathematical foundations for recovering spacetime dimension from causal structure.

**Rafael Sorkin**: American physicist, professor emeritus at Syracuse University and visiting fellow at the Perimeter Institute. Principal architect of causal set theory. Coined the slogan Order plus Number equals Geometry. Also known for proposing entanglement entropy as the origin of black hole entropy.

**Luca Bombelli**: Italian physicist, now at the University of Mississippi. Co-author of the foundational nineteen eighty seven paper establishing causal sets as an approach to quantum gravity.

**David Meyer**: American physicist at the University of California San Diego. Co-author of the nineteen eighty seven paper and contributor to the mathematical foundations of the theory.

**Joohan Lee**: South Korean physicist who contributed to the original nineteen eighty seven formulation of causal set theory.

**Fay Dowker**: British theoretical physicist at Imperial College London. Former student of Stephen Hawking. Developer with Dionigi Benincasa of the discrete analog of the Einstein-Hilbert action. Prominent advocate for causal sets and frequent public communicator of the ideas.

**David Rideout**: Physicist who collaborated with Sorkin on classical sequential growth dynamics, providing a framework for causet evolution.

**Sumati Surya**: Physicist at the Raman Research Institute in India. Contributor to understanding spacetime topology recovery from causal sets.
